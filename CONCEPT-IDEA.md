# Concept idea: Flood-risk-aware homebuying in New York City (inferred title)

## Problem

Homebuyers may pay too much for a home if its price does not account for relevant flood risk. The project's founder saw the aftermath of Superstorm Sandy in Red Hook, Brooklyn, and now sees property listings there whose prices, in the founder's view, do not reflect the area's flood risk.

The founder believes the risks may be greater than current estimates suggest and that climate change is increasing them over time. These beliefs have not yet been established by research for this project.

## Who has this problem

Prospective homebuyers in New York City's five boroughs who want to pay a fair price for a property they may own for many years. The intended users may have limited knowledge of climate change's history, present-day effects, and possible future effects, but are interested in understanding a property's flood risk before buying.

## Current alternatives and evidence

- FEMA flood maps are available.
- Homeowners can obtain insurance policies that protect against losses.
- The founder's motivating observation is personal: seeing standing water, mud, and residue in Red Hook the morning after Superstorm Sandy, and later seeing listings there whose prices seem not to reflect flood risk.

Whether buyers use these maps or insurance information to evaluate and price flood risk, and whether listings are in fact underpricing that risk, remain unverified.

## Proposed concept

A tool focused exclusively on the five boroughs of New York City. A prospective buyer could search by address or property listing, enter a listed sale price, and receive information and documented support about the property's flood risk and how much that risk may or may not be reflected in its price.

The proposed interface could include a heat map showing flood-risk levels alongside residential real-estate pricing trends, to show where there may be greater value at risk. The tool is meant to help buyers inform their own decisions and negotiations; it does not decide for them what a property is worth (inferred).

## First-version boundaries

- Cover only the five boroughs of New York City.
- Provide information and documented support for prospective buyers to use in their own decisions and negotiations.
- Do not provide an official appraisal or guarantee that a price is correct.
- Do not advise users on insurance or lending.
- Do not negotiate or contact sellers.
- Do not act on a buyer's behalf (inferred).

## What success looks like

The long-term intended impact is to shift New York City real-estate prices so they reflect relevant historical and projected future flood risks.

For this fall semester, the goal is to prove the concept with a minimum viable product that others can view, tinker with, improve, and expand. No numeric target has been set yet.

## Constraints and collaborators

- Work is planned for the rest of the fall semester.
- The project should be completed without spending money.
- Several collaborators and an advisor are working on similar projects.

## Open questions

- Which authoritative sources can provide historical flood events, current flood exposure, and future flood projections at a useful address or neighborhood level?
- How should flood-risk levels be defined and explained, and how will the map distinguish recorded history from projections?
- What method can estimate whether flood risk is reflected in a property's asking price, and how should uncertainty be shown?
- What data is available for residential listing prices and historical price trends in the five boroughs? Can it be used without a paid subscription or prohibited scraping?
- Will users enter address and listing price manually, or will the MVP connect to a listing source? What listing sources permit the intended use?
- What documents and supporting evidence would buyers find useful in their own seller or lender conversations, without turning the tool into lending advice?
- How can the team test whether homebuyers understand and trust the risk information?
- What measurable semester milestone would demonstrate progress toward the long-term goal?
- How will collaborators and the advisor divide work and review assumptions?
